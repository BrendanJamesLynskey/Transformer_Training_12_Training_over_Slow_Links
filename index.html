A Toy, and Where the Real Measurements Are

Read this first

The runs in this deck are a toy: Part 1’s 5,376-parameter character model, 4 simulated workers in one process, each training on 4 of the default run’s 16 windows per step, 2,000 steps, one book, a CPU, three seeds per setting. They show the mechanisms running on a model small enough to follow. They do not say what works at scale.

The Compute in Transit results site measured the same ideas on a 15.8-million-parameter GPT trained by eight simulated workers for 3,000 steps on TinyStories, on GPUs, and then simulated data-centre-scale links with those measurements. Its numbers, not these, are the evidence; this deck explains the concepts its pages rely on. Where the two disagree (they do: slide 08), believe the results site.

WAN and Inter-Data-Centre Bandwidth

Inside a server, NVLink moves hundreds of gigabytes a second per GPU; across a data centre’s network, tens (deck 10). A wide-area network (WAN) link between data centres is shared by a whole site and can offer each training job far less: a 100 GbE link is 12.5 GB/s for everything that crosses it.

NVLink (H100)

450 GB/s each way per GPU

Cluster network (H100)

50 GB/s per GPU

100 GbE WAN

12.5 GB/s for the whole site

At those rates, step time = compute + communication is dominated by the communication: LLaMA 7B’s dense BF16 gradient (13.5 GB) takes over a second to send at 12.5 GB/s, every step, before any other job shares the link. Training over slow links means sending less, sending less often, or both.

03

Top-k Sparsification

Send only the \(k\) largest values of a vector, and where they are: here 1 in 16 per tensor. Most of a gradient’s coordinates are small at any one step, so most of the step survives.

Positions cost bytes

Each kept value needs its position: for Part 1’s model 2 bytes of BF16 value plus 2 bytes of position, so 1 value in 16 costs 4/32 of the dense bytes, not 1/16: 1,344 bytes a step against 10,752 for the dense gradient.

Deep Gradient Compression

Lin et al. (2018) apply it to an accumulated momentum: each worker adds its gradient into a local momentum and an accumulator, sends the top of the accumulator, and plain SGD steps with the average of what was sent (momentum 0.9 here).

$$\begin{gathered}u_i \leftarrow \mu u_i + g_i,\qquad v_i \leftarrow v_i + u_i \\ s_i = \operatorname{top}_k(v_i),\qquad v_i \leftarrow v_i - s_i \\ \theta \leftarrow \theta - \tfrac{\eta}{W}\textstyle\sum_i s_i\end{gathered}$$
04

Error Feedback: Keep the Residual

What a compressor drops is not thrown away: each worker keeps it, the residual (\(v_i - s_i\) above), and adds it to the next step’s vector before compressing again. A coordinate too small to be sent this step keeps growing until it is large enough to be sent, so every update arrives eventually, only late.

Without it, the steady small coordinates of the gradient would never be applied at all; with it, compressed SGD provably converges at the uncompressed rate in the long run (Stich et al. 2018, sparsified SGD with memory; Karimireddy et al. 2019, error feedback fixes signSGD). 1-bit SGD (Seide et al. 2014) had used the same trick to send one bit per gradient value. DeMo’s momentum is its residual.

05

Why Compressed Vectors Are All-Gathered

A ring all-reduce adds the workers’ chunks as they pass, which needs every worker’s vector to have the same layout (deck 10). Top-k vectors don’t: each worker picks its own positions. So the workers all-gather instead: every worker receives every other worker’s values and positions and adds them itself.

$$\begin{gathered}\text{dense: } 2\,\tfrac{W-1}{W}\,2P \qquad \text{compressed: } (W-1)\,c \\ \text{DiLoCo: } 2\,\tfrac{W-1}{W}\,2P\,/\,H\end{gathered}$$

Bytes on the wire per worker per step for \(W\) workers, \(P\) parameters, 2-byte values and a compressed payload of \(c\) bytes. With four workers DeMo’s 1,008 bytes of payload become 3,024 on the wire, against the dense all-reduce’s 16,128. With eight workers the same payload costs 7 times itself, which is why the results site quotes both a payload ratio and a smaller wire ratio. Try it on slide 09.

06

DeMo: Decoupled Momentum

Peng et al. (2024) let each worker keep its own momentum instead of syncing gradients (their Algorithm 1):

  1. fold the gradient into the local momentum (β = 0.999 here);
  2. cut each tensor into chunks (up to 64 values) and take each chunk’s discrete cosine transform;
  3. send the \(k\) largest coefficients per chunk (4 of 64 here) and subtract them from the momentum: the rest stays behind as error feedback;
  4. everyone averages the coefficients gathered, transforms back, and steps by the sign of the result.

The DCT rearranges a chunk into frequency components without losing anything (it is orthonormal: what is sent and what is kept add up to the whole), and the momentum’s large, slowly changing components tend to collect in a few coefficients, the “fast components” that get sent. Values in BF16 and positions in one byte: 1,008 bytes a step per worker.

07

DiLoCo: Sync Every H Steps

The other way to save bytes is to sync less often. In DiLoCo (Douillard et al. 2023, Algorithm 1) each worker trains alone with AdamW for \(H\) steps; then the workers average how far they moved (the pseudo-gradient) and an outer optimiser, Nesterov momentum (learning rate 0.7, momentum 0.9, the paper’s setting), moves the shared weights; then everyone starts the next \(H\) steps from them.

$$\begin{gathered}\Delta = \tfrac{1}{W}\textstyle\sum_i (\theta - \theta_i), \qquad b \leftarrow \mu b + \Delta \\ \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta_{\text{out}}(\Delta + \mu b)\end{gathered}$$

It sends the dense vector, but only once per \(H\) steps: 1,613 bytes a step on average at \(H = 10\), 323 at \(H = 50\). Local SGD, its ancestor, is analysed by Stich (2018).

Queueing on a Shared Link, and Stragglers

Queueing

A shared WAN carries other traffic; a sync that arrives while the link is busy waits. With transfers arriving at random and the link busy a fraction \(\rho\) of the time, a newcomer waits on average \(\rho\,s/(2(1-\rho))\) for transfers of length \(s\) (M/D/1, the Pollaczek–Khinchine formula). The wait grows without bound as the link fills, and hurts most the methods that send most.

Stragglers

Synchronous training waits for the slowest worker every step. Eight workers, each step 1 + 0.1×Exp(1) time units: syncing every step costs the slowest of eight; syncing every \(H\) steps, as DiLoCo does, waits for the slowest sum of \(H\) steps, which is closer to the mean.

What to Take Away

  • Over a WAN, communication dominates the step: send less (top-k, DeMo) or less often (DiLoCo).
  • Error feedback keeps what was not sent, so it arrives later instead of never.
  • Compressed vectors have different layouts per worker, so they are all-gathered: wire bytes \((W-1)\times\) payload, growing with the workers.
  • DeMo: DCT of momentum chunks, send the fast components, sign step. DiLoCo: \(H\) local AdamW steps, then an outer Nesterov step on the pseudo-gradient.
  • Queues and stragglers add waiting that bandwidth alone does not show.
  • This deck’s runs are a toy; the evidence is on the Compute in Transit results site.
